01 · The idea

Tension is distance from home. Release is arriving.

Every chord has a home: its root, its 3rd and its 5th. Play one of those and the note sounds settled. Step away from home and the ear leans forward, waiting for you to come back. The farther you step and the sharper the clash, the harder the pull.

Release is the moment the note arrives. Players build the pull, hold it a beat longer than feels comfortable, then land on the root, 3rd or 5th. The landing feels big because of the build.

1

Release

The root, 3rd and 5th. These notes sit at rest and make good places to land.

2

Color

The 7th and the scale notes that sit comfortably around the chord, such as the 9th and the 6th. They sweeten a line and still sound settled.

3

Tension

A scale note a half step above a chord tone, such as the 4th over a major or dominant chord. It clashes with the note below it and wants to drop onto it.

4

Outside

Notes that are not in the chord's scale at all. They pull the hardest. Blues players call the flat 3rd over a dominant chord a blue note, and they bend it up into the major 3rd.

One example. Over C7 the notes are C, E, G and Bb. F sits a fret above E, so F rubs against the 3rd. Play F, hold it, and drop to E. That small drop is a release. The same F over an F chord is the root, home, and carries no tension at all. Tension belongs to the chord underneath the note.

06 · The long arc

Five dials you can turn

Notes are one way to build tension. Every other part of your playing can climb too. A solo builds when several dials rise together, and it releases when they all drop at the same moment you land.

1

Distance from the chord

Start with chord tones, add color, then lean on tension and outside notes. Each step moves farther from home.

2

Rhythm

Long notes feel calm. Quarter notes, then eighths, then sixteenths feel like speeding up even when the tempo holds still.

3

Register

Higher notes sound more urgent. Climb a few frets at a time and save the highest notes for the peak.

4

Volume and attack

Pick harder, bend wider and let the sustain ring. Softer playing at the landing makes the release feel earned.

5

Repetition

Repeat one short phrase and squeeze it tighter each time. The ear waits for the pattern to break, and the break is your release.

07 · What to listen for

Trey and Rick

These are listening prompts to try on your favorite long jams. They describe what the ear can pick up and make no claim to be transcriptions.

Trey Anastasio

Put on a long Phish jam and follow one phrase. Notice how he repeats a short idea while the band lifts underneath him, then settles on a clear note as the section turns. Ask which note he landed on and what he played two bars earlier. The bends from the flat 3rd up to the 3rd are blues vocabulary for exactly this kind of pull.

Rick Mitarotonda

In a Guitar Player interview Rick describes a Sonny Rollins idea: keep pushing, then get out of the way and let the music carry the band. In this lesson's terms the push is the build and getting out of the way is the release. Listen for the point where his line stops pushing and settles while the band keeps moving.

09 · The pattern recognition

Four rules that carry to any song

A fret away pulls hardest

A note one fret from a chord tone wants to move to it. Spot the nearest chord tone and you know where the line is heading.

The 4th leans down

Over a major or dominant chord, the 4th sits a fret above the 3rd. F over C7 drops to E. Hold the 4th for a beat, then let it fall.

The blue note leans up

The flat 3rd over a major or dominant chord, Eb over C7, bends or slides up to the major 3rd. The flat 5th leans up to the 5th.

The chord decides the job

The same note changes role when the chord changes. F is tension over C7 and the root over F7. Landing on a note that was tense a bar earlier makes the change feel like a payoff.

10 · Put it to work

Practice path

  1. Load the 12-bar blues. Pick C7 and find every release note in the window, then check yourself with the release-only box.
  2. Play each tension note in the table, hold it for two beats and resolve it to the target. Say the interval out loud.
  3. Run the drills for C7, then F7, then G7. Notice how the same fret shape changes job on each chord.
  4. Play one four-bar line from the plan. Start with long chord tones, climb through the build and land on the 3rd of the release bar.
  5. Pick a song you know, type in its chords and play along at a slow tempo. Build for three bars and land on the change.

11 · Quick answers

Questions about tension and release on guitar

What is tension and release in a guitar solo?

Tension is a note or a rhythm that pulls forward, and release is the arrival that relaxes the pull. Soloists build tension with notes that clash with the chord, then release it by landing on the root, 3rd or 5th.

Which notes create tension?

Notes a half step above a chord tone and notes outside the chord's scale create the most tension. Over a dominant chord that includes the 4th, the flat 3rd and the flat 5th.

Which notes give release?

The root, 3rd and 5th of the current chord. The 3rd is the most descriptive, the root feels most final and the 5th sounds open.

How long should I hold the tension?

Hold it until the ear begins to ask for the resolution, usually one to four beats. Release on a strong beat such as beat 1 of a new bar or a chord change.

Can I build tension without outside notes?

Yes. Rhythm, register, volume and repetition all build tension with in-scale notes. Playing the same short phrase faster and higher, then landing on a chord tone, works with any notes.

How is this different from playing the 3rd?

Lesson 1 shows where each 3rd lives. This lesson gives the 3rd a job as the landing spot, and the notes before it supply the pull.
